What Is PGP and How Does It Work?

By OnlinePGP Editorial Team • Published November 23, 2026
Illustration: Flat illustration of two people exchanging encrypted messages using public and private PGP keys

Introduction to Pretty Good Privacy (PGP)

Pretty Good Privacy (PGP) is a cryptographic system created in 1991 by Phil Zimmermann to provide privacy and authentication for digital communications. Originally designed to protect email messages and files from unauthorized interception, PGP has evolved into an open standard known as OpenPGP, defined by the IETF in RFC 4880 and updated in RFC 9580.

At its core, PGP solves two fundamental problems of internet communication: confidentiality (ensuring only the intended recipient can read a message) and authenticity (proving who actually sent a message and that it was not altered in transit). Whether you are a journalist protecting a source, a developer signing software releases, or an everyday user who values privacy, understanding PGP gives you direct control over your data.

Unlike proprietary messaging apps that lock you into a single platform, PGP is an interoperable standard. A message encrypted with our online PGP tool can be decrypted in Thunderbird, GnuPG on Linux, or Proton Mail, because all of these tools speak the same OpenPGP format.

Symmetric vs. Asymmetric Cryptography

To understand how PGP works, it helps to contrast two types of encryption: symmetric and asymmetric. In symmetric encryption, the same secret key is used to both lock (encrypt) and unlock (decrypt) a message. Symmetric algorithms like AES (Advanced Encryption Standard) are very fast and secure, but they have a major drawback: how do you safely share the secret key with someone you have never met in person?

Asymmetric cryptography—also called public-key cryptography—solves this key-distribution problem by using a mathematically linked pair of keys: a public key and a private key. Anything encrypted with a person's public key can only be decrypted by their corresponding private key. You can publish your public key on your website, in your email signature, or on a keyserver without compromising your security.

However, asymmetric algorithms like RSA are computationally slower than symmetric algorithms, especially for large files or long emails. That is why PGP combines both approaches into a clever design called hybrid encryption.

How PGP Hybrid Encryption Works Step by Step

When you encrypt a message in your browser using PGP, the software does not encrypt your entire text directly with RSA. Instead, it follows a fast, multi-step process:

First, PGP generates a random, one-time symmetric session key. Second, it encrypts your plaintext message using that session key with a fast symmetric cipher such as AES-256. Third, it encrypts the small session key itself using the recipient's public key. Finally, it bundles the encrypted message and the encrypted session key together into a single ASCII-armored text block starting with -----BEGIN PGP MESSAGE-----.

When the recipient receives this block, their PGP software uses their private key (unlocked by their passphrase) to decrypt the session key, and then uses that session key to decrypt the full message. This gives you the speed of symmetric encryption with the convenience of public-key distribution.

Digital Signatures: Proving Identity and Integrity

Encryption hides the contents of a message, but it does not prove who sent it—after all, anyone with your public key can encrypt a message to you. To prove authorship, PGP uses digital signatures.

When you sign a message, PGP calculates a cryptographic hash (a unique digital fingerprint) of your text and encrypts that hash with your private key. Anyone who has your public key can verify the signature. If even a single character in the message is changed after signing, the hash will no longer match and verification will fail.

Digital signatures are widely used in software development, security advisories, and journalism. You can test this yourself by using the Sign & Verify section on OnlinePGP.com.

ASCII Armor and Key Fingerprints

Raw PGP data is binary, which can get corrupted when pasted into web forms or plain-text emails. To solve this, PGP uses ASCII Armor—a Base64 encoding wrapped in clear header and footer lines such as -----BEGIN PGP PUBLIC KEY BLOCK-----. This makes keys and encrypted messages easy to copy, paste, and store.

Every PGP public key also has a unique 40-character hexadecimal fingerprint. Verifying a contact's fingerprint through a trusted channel—such as a phone call or in-person meeting—ensures you are encrypting to the real person and not an impostor. Frequently Asked Questions

Is PGP still secure today?

Yes. When used with modern key sizes such as RSA-2048/4096 or Curve25519 (Ed25519/X25519) and a strong passphrase, OpenPGP remains cryptographically sound.

What is the difference between a public key and a private key?

Your public key is shared openly so others can encrypt messages to you or verify your signatures. Your private key must be kept secret and is used to decrypt messages and create signatures.

Can I use PGP without installing software?

Yes. You can generate a PGP key online and encrypt or decrypt messages directly in your browser using client-side OpenPGP.js tools like OnlinePGP.com.
